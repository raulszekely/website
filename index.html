I am a research psychologist working at the intersection of the social and behavioural sciences and human-computer interaction. My research examines how digital tools and interventions, spanning web platforms, mobile applications, virtual reality and, increasingly, conversational agents, can be designed and evaluated to support health, learning and wellbeing in ways that are accessible, equitable and inclusive.

Methodologically, I adopt a participatory, user-centred stance, combining evidence synthesis, qualitative and mixed-methods inquiry, experimental designs and co-design with intended users and stakeholders. I am particularly concerned with populations that digital health has historically underserved, including disabled and neurodivergent people, ethnic minority communities, and those affected by socioeconomic disadvantage or digital exclusion.

At the University of Roehampton, I lead the institution's contribution to Together for Mental Health (Together4MH), a Horizon Europe-funded project developing an evidence-based, co-created toolbox for mental health promotion across educational and workplace settings. This work involves the translation of research evidence into accessible resources, participatory adaptation with young people, students and educators, and an implementation case study in UK secondary schools. Previously, at Queen Mary University of London, I contributed to the NIHR-funded development of Diabetes Balance, a web-based intervention for diabetes-related distress, leading the evidence synthesis, co-leading participatory design and usability testing, and devising a behaviourally informed engagement strategy.

My doctoral research at the University of Surrey investigated immersive virtual reality as a means of reducing mental illness stigma among healthcare students. Drawing on experiences such as Goliath: Playing with Reality (Anagram, 2021), I examined both its efficacy and the mechanisms and design features through which attitudinal change occurs. This has informed a broader interest in media representations of mental illness and their role in shaping public understanding. More recently, my work has turned to engagement with large language model-based conversational agents in mental health contexts, with particular attention to trust, stigma and safety.

I have also contributed to the development of the Mental Health Technology Assessment of Quality (MTAQ), a stakeholder-informed framework for the systematic quality assurance of digital mental health and wellbeing tools. I teach and supervise across undergraduate and postgraduate psychology, review for leading journals in digital health and human-computer interaction, and am committed to communicating research to audiences beyond academia.
