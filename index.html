About

I am a psychologist and researcher interested in how people experience, understand and engage with technology, particularly where it intersects with mental health. My work brings together psychology, digital health and emerging technologies — both the opportunities these bring, and the psychological, ethical and practical questions that come with them.

I completed my PhD in Applied Psychology at the University of Surrey, examining whether virtual reality could reduce mental health stigma among healthcare students. Before that, I completed an MSc in Organisational Psychiatry & Psychology at King's College London and a BSc in Psychology at Brunel University.

I am interested in research that is theoretically informed, methodologically rigorous and useful in practice. Depending on the question, I draw on established models and approaches, among others: the COM-B model (Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behaviour) and Behaviour Change Wheel, which explain and help change behaviour; UTAUT (the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology), which explains why people take up new technologies; the Person-Based Approach; and user-centred design. Where research involves people and technology, I place particular weight on understanding people's experiences, involving relevant users and stakeholders — including neurodivergent and disabled people — and attending to inclusion, accessibility, ethics and responsible practice.

Research

My research examines psychological questions arising from the development and use of digital and emerging technologies, with a particular focus on mental health.

  • Digital mental health

    How digital technologies can support mental health, and how interventions can be built around people's needs.

  • Virtual reality & immersive technologies

    The psychological applications and implications of immersive experience.

  • Artificial intelligence & mental health

    How people interact with AI-based technologies, and how these should be developed and evaluated.

  • Inclusive digital health

    Using qualitative research, user testing and co-design — including with neurodivergent and disabled people — to inform more inclusive technologies.

  • Engagement with digital interventions

    Using behavioural science to understand why engagement drops off, and how design can respond to real user experience.

Consultancy

I work with organisations developing, evaluating or researching digital and emerging technologies in mental health and healthcare — advising on a research question, carrying out the research directly, or assessing the evidence behind an existing technology.

Research advice & strategy

For teams that know roughly what they want to find out, but want an experienced researcher to help shape the approach.

  • Framing research questions and study design
  • Choosing methods for a qualitative or mixed-methods project
  • Designing research protocols and evaluation plans
  • Interpreting findings and their implications
  • Ongoing input across a research or development project

Research & evaluation

For teams that want the research carried out directly, from a single defined activity to a full evaluation.

  • Co-design with users and stakeholders
  • Usability research — user testing, think-aloud studies and interviews
  • Effectiveness research — outcome evaluations, feasibility studies and pilot trials
  • Using behavioural science to diagnose engagement problems and design targeted improvements
  • Qualitative, quantitative and mixed-methods analysis
  • Research reports with clear recommendations

Evidence & quality

For teams that need to know whether a technology's evidence holds up — including for regulatory, commissioning or market-access purposes.

  • Literature reviews, including systematic and scoping reviews
  • Evidence synthesis and mapping
  • Quality assessment of digital health tools
  • Identifying gaps the evidence hasn't yet addressed
  • Evidence-informed recommendations

Ethics & responsible practice

All three are grounded in the same principles: protecting participant wellbeing, being clear about what people are consenting to, handling data carefully, and being honest about what a technology's evidence does and doesn't show.
